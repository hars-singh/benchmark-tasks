# Manual feed and crank protocol

This task preserves simultaneous axial pencil feeding and crank rotation. The moving base supplies a repeatable disturbance; the motion is not the claim of a new physical skill.

A conventional manually fed crank sharpener has a gear-driven cutting member. Its pencil must be held and fed while the crank is rotated. The prior-art description in [US20110226383A1](https://patents.google.com/patent/US20110226383A1/en), paragraphs 0003–0005, distinguishes this mechanism from automatic-feed designs. The simulation represents the manual-feed mechanism. Automatic-feed Dahle/CARL designs are not equivalent substitutes for this procedure.

For inexpensive real evaluation, a commercial manual-feed sharpener such as the X-ACTO KS 1031 can be desk-mounted on the moving plate. The [retailer listing](https://www.michaels.com/product/x-acto-manual-pencil-sharpener-D230835S) identifies its desk/wall mounting and dual helical cutters. This is an example source for an ordinary working tool, not a claim that this simulation reproduces that product's dimensions or measured cutting loads. Its housing and crank geometry must be measured before a real-trial comparison. The included SCAD is the simulated fixture's dimensional reference; printed plastic parts alone do not make a working sharpener.

The numerical simulation uses a 500 N/m cutter-face spring with 6 mm travel. A displacement of at least 2 mm represents at least 1 N feed resistance. Passive crank damping is 0.020 N m s/rad, capped at 0.08 N m: at the demonstration's four-second revolution, nominal resistance is approximately 0.031 N m. These are declared surrogate loads, not experimentally measured properties of a commercial model. Native contacts and the passive joints supply them. No environmental motor turns the crank or moves the pencil.

Eight net clockwise revolutions while aligned, inserted and loaded trigger a material-removal approximation. Either visually identical blunt end can be used; progress is tracked separately for each, so four turns on each end do not count as eight on one end. The full-length pencil collider remains intact; only its appearance changes to a 20 mm wooden cone with a 3 mm graphite point at the end actually fed. Shavings, grain-dependent fracture and cutter-tooth geometry are omitted. The preserved collider overestimates the sharpened tip's contact cross-section. The two return rests contact the uncut portion of the pencil. Judge a real outcome from an intact visible graphite point and returned pencil, rather than assuming eight revolutions always produce the same quality across pencils and cutters. The simulation's eight-turn rule is a proxy for that material response.

Use a fresh blunt 140 mm long, 7 mm diameter round pencil per real trial. The guide carriage starts in its centre and moves 5 mm either side in Y with an eight-second period. Both arms start READY with open grippers. Keep the commercial cutter enclosed. End after 80 simulated seconds or one second of sustained full score; inspect the returned pencil from the side and above. Reset the carriage, remove shavings and replace the pencil. No live human hand is in this fixture.

## Comparison with existing manipulation tasks

The benchmark board snapshot reviewed during development contained 722 tasks. No title, keyword or instruction matched pencil sharpening. Generic handwheel turns do not require keeping a second loose workpiece fed into a cutter under axial load; screw tightening uses wrist reorientation and threaded engagement rather than a free-spinning crank knob with simultaneous independent workpiece feed. The task retains the latter coordination and scores empty cranking as zero.

The published [BEHAVIOR task index](https://behavior.stanford.edu/knowledgebase/tasks/) did not contain sharpening or a pencil sharpener when checked. The [DexJoCo task gallery](https://dexjoco.github.io/) includes assembly, screwdriving, pressing and handheld camera use, but does not list this operation. This comparison supports a candidate for review; it is not an assertion of acceptance or an exhaustive absence claim across all robotics research.
